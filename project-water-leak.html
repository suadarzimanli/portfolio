Water Leak Monitoring Dashboard

A teaching dashboard and simulation study on leak alarms: with every detector held to the same rate of wasted crew trips, a context check using only what an operator knows lifts leaks caught from 83–90% to 97% and leak water saved from 86–88% to 93%.

Overview

What this project does

Eight pressure zones report hourly flow and pressure with weekday and weekend demand and weather. Hidden leaks run until a crew repairs them or customers report them, alongside events that look like leaks: planned hydrant tests and valve work (published in a schedule that is incomplete and sometimes wrong), unscheduled hydrant use, drifting pressure sensors and hot-weather demand. Rules, a label-free EWMA control chart and a logistic regression, each with and without a context check, are held to the same false-alert budget and compared by leaks caught and leak water saved. In the dashboard the operator decides whether to dispatch a crew; the crew's report two hours later reveals what the simulation contained.

Research questions

What I set out to answer

  • At the same false-alert budget, how do threshold rules, a label-free EWMA chart and a learned model compare in leaks caught, delay and water saved?
  • How much does a context check using only operator-visible information add, and does it survive an unreliable maintenance schedule?
  • How much does a learned detector depend on label quality and on representative training data?
  • How can a dashboard teach the path from alarm to crew report without showing what an operator could not know?

Key findings

What the study showed

  • At the same false-alert rate, rules, EWMA and ML alone caught 328–353 of 393 leaks and saved 86–88% of the leak water; with the context check, 381–382 leaks and 93%.
  • With context, the label-free EWMA chart was as good as the supervised model; trained on realistic labels (the hours before a customer report), the model caught only 142 leaks.
  • The gain held when only half the planned work was announced, with noisier sensors, smaller leaks and twice as many look-alike events.
